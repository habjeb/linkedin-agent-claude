# Writing rules: notes that read like a person wrote them

A connection note has one job: make a busy person think "this sender actually looked at my business" and answer one easy question. Generic or machine-sounding notes get ignored, and repetitive templated notes sent in volume are one of the signals platforms use to flag accounts. Human writing is both more effective and safer.

## Structure (keep it, vary the wording)

1. **Greeting with first name.** "Bonjour Alain," / "Hi Sarah," (formal titles where the culture expects them, for example "Maître" for French lawyers).
2. **One verified, specific detail** about them or their business. Something only they have: a number, a date, an event, a place, a concrete observation about their site.
3. **The offer, in one plain sentence,** in first person ("I build…", "Je développe…"). Lead with the main offer.
4. **The secondary offer only if they have the matching need,** tied to what you saw ("your site still shows © 2017").
5. **One simple question.** Easy to answer in one line.

Total: 2 to 4 short sentences, within the note limit shown by LinkedIn.

## Must

- Write the way the sender would talk to someone at a trade fair: plain, direct, a bit warm.
- Use short sentences. One idea per sentence.
- Use the prospect's own words for their business when you have them (their club name, their product name).
- Keep every factual claim checkable.
- Vary the opening, the sentence order and the closing question across the batch. No two notes in a batch should share the same first five words, and no more than two should share the same closing question.

## Never

**Stock phrases** (any language):
- French: "J'espère que vous allez bien", "Je me permets de vous contacter", "N'hésitez pas", "solution innovante", "clé en main", "booster", "révolutionner", "à l'ère du digital", "dans un monde où", "je serais ravi d'échanger", "valeur ajoutée", "synergie".
- English: "I hope this finds you well", "I came across your profile and was impressed", "reach out", "touch base", "game-changer", "cutting-edge", "unlock", "leverage", "seamless", "elevate", "in today's fast-paced world", "I'd love to pick your brain".

**AI tells:**
- Em-dashes or spaced hyphens used for asides. Use a period or a comma instead.
- Lists of three adjectives ("simple, fast and powerful").
- "Not just X, but Y" constructions.
- Colon-then-reveal sentences ("The secret: …").
- Vague compliments ("Impressive journey!", "Great profile!").
- Exclamation marks in more than one sentence.
- Emojis, unless the user's config asks for them, and never more than one.
- Perfectly parallel sentences across the batch.

**Sales pitfalls:**
- Naming or criticizing the prospect's current provider or software. Describe the benefit instead.
- Prices, discounts or "free trial" in the first note.
- Links in the first note.
- Pretending to know them, or inventing a shared context ("as we discussed").
- Mentioning the company brand when the config says to use only the product brand.

## Examples

Weak (generic, templated):

> Bonjour Jean, j'espère que vous allez bien. Je me permets de vous contacter car nous proposons une solution innovante et clé en main pour les clubs de padel. N'hésitez pas si vous souhaitez en savoir plus !

Good (specific, plain, one question):

> Bonjour Jean, 6 pistes couvertes et un tournoi chaque premier samedi du mois, ça tourne bien chez vous ! Je développe une app de réservation et de caisse aux couleurs du club, sans commission. Qu'utilisez-vous aujourd'hui pour le planning ?

Good, with a secondary offer because the need is visible:

> Hi Sarah, congrats on opening the second shop in Leeds. I build ordering apps for independent bakeries, branded as your own. I also noticed your site still lists the old opening hours; happy to fix that too. Worth a quick chat?

## Self-review before showing the batch

Read every note and check:

1. Does it contain one detail only this prospect has, and did I verify it?
2. Is the main offer clear in one sentence?
3. Is the secondary offer there only if the need is real?
4. Any stock phrase, em-dash aside, triple adjective or second exclamation mark? Rewrite.
5. Is it within the character limit, counted exactly?
6. Read the batch top to bottom: do the openings and questions vary? If two notes look alike, rewrite one.
7. Would a busy owner believe a person typed this after looking at their business? If not, rewrite.
